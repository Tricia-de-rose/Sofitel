Modern Slavery Prevention & Ethical Supply Chain Strategy

01 · Project Overview

The business challenge: translating commitments into controls

Original client objectives

  1. Understand the role of UN Sustainable Development Goals in hotel operations.
  2. Identify the goals most relevant to modern slavery prevention.
  3. Examine potential gaps between procurement practice and responsible sourcing objectives.
  4. Recommend feasible improvements for supplier governance and monitoring.

Luxury hospitality relies on diverse supplier relationships: food, linen, technology, cleaning, security, maintenance and agency labour. The consulting question was how to strengthen oversight without creating an impractical burden on hotel operations.

Consulting scope and contribution

The project involved interpreting the client brief, investigating supplier governance issues, assessing sourcing categories, connecting findings to ESG frameworks and translating research into structured recommendations. My portfolio highlights analytical reasoning, research synthesis and implementation planning rather than claiming that I managed the hotel's procurement function.

Key research distinction

A signed supplier declaration, a preferred-supplier designation and independently verified working conditions are not equivalent forms of evidence. This distinction shaped the recommendation to use layered assurance: initial screening, supporting documentation, targeted verification and ongoing corrective-action monitoring.

The analysis identified potential vulnerabilities and improvement opportunities; it did not establish that modern slavery occurred at the hotel.

Recurring governance themes

  • Accountability: responsibilities may be distributed across ownership, brand-level and property-level structures.
  • Screening: supplier pathways may not apply equivalent due diligence.
  • Upstream visibility: direct contracts may reveal little about subcontractors or lower tiers.
  • Assurance: self-declarations alone cannot verify working conditions.
  • Monitoring: periodic reviews and escalation processes are needed.
04 · Strategic Recommendations

Three pillars of a practical supplier governance programme

The proposed framework integrates supplier information, management responsibility and evidence-based assurance. It is a consolidated consulting recommendation, not a programme confirmed as adopted by the client.

01

Visibility & Transparency

Establish a central supplier register with category, sourcing pathway, available documentation, review dates and risk indicators. Standardise questionnaires and progressively request upstream subcontractor information.

Supplier recordsRisk segmentationTraceability
02

Governance & Accountability

Assign responsible owners, standardise onboarding, integrate ethical sourcing requirements into contracts and define exception approvals, escalation routes and recurring management reviews.

Clear ownershipContract controlsEscalation
03

Assurance & Improvement

Combine documentary checks with targeted independent verification, worker-centred reporting channels, corrective-action monitoring and periodic reassessment for priority suppliers.

Evidence checksWorker voiceRemediation

Proposed technology support

Digital ESG assessment platforms, document review tools and supplier-monitoring dashboards could make records more accessible and reviews easier to coordinate. The project considered platforms such as EcoVadis, Sedex and Certainty Software as options for assessment and tracking, while recognising cost and verification limitations.

Automated document screening and red–amber–green triage were explored as concepts, not functioning AI systems developed or deployed during the engagement.

Worker protection and verification

Contractual provisions could support audit access, subcontractor disclosure, cooperation with corrective actions and confidential worker feedback. Where appropriate, independent interviews, multilingual surveys and grievance mechanisms would help ensure that assurance does not depend solely on supplier self-reporting.

Commercial feasibility matters: a phased, risk-based approach is more practical than attempting to audit every supplier immediately.

Implementation considerations

  • Start with Tier 1 suppliers and labour-sensitive categories.
  • Use existing management structures before adding complex systems.
  • Prioritise missing documentation and higher-risk pathways.
  • Define accountable owners and review intervals.
  • Use targeted independent verification where justified.
  • Track corrective actions to closure rather than treating screening as a one-off exercise.
07 · Key Insights & Business Relevance

What the consulting engagement demonstrates

Policy needs operational ownership

High-level sustainability commitments become more meaningful when translated into procurement controls, named responsibilities, documentation requirements and review processes.

Risk-based controls are more feasible

Supplier segmentation helps organisations focus limited resources where labour exposure, subcontracting complexity and weak visibility may warrant additional review.

Verification matters

Declarations are useful starting points, but credible due diligence depends on appropriate evidence, independent checks, worker feedback and monitored remediation.

Data supports accountability

Supplier registers, screening status, documentation gaps and corrective-action KPIs can turn a complex governance problem into measurable management activity.

Commercial context shapes strategy

Recommendations must balance human-rights safeguards with cost, operational continuity, supplier relationships and the practical constraints of hospitality procurement.

My professional learning

This engagement strengthened my ability to connect research, analytical frameworks, stakeholder considerations and implementation planning into practical business recommendations.
